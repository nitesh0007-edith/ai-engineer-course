You watch a three-hour video on transformers. You nod along. It makes sense. A week later a colleague asks why attention divides by the square root of the key dimension, and you have nothing — not because you are slow, but because **watching is not the same as knowing**, and your brain quietly filed the video under "seen," not "learned."

That gap — between recognising an idea when you see it and being able to reproduce it when you need it — is where most self-taught AI engineers get stuck. This chapter is about how this course is built to close that gap, and what you have to do for it to work.

## The intuition

The whole course rests on one unglamorous idea: **you learn engineering by doing engineering.** Not by reading about it. So every chapter contains at least one thing you cannot get from prose — something you can run, edit, break, and watch respond.

The cells in this course are not screenshots. They are real Python, executed in your browser by <Term id="pyodide">Pyodide</Term>, with nothing sent to a server. When a demo genuinely needs a hosted model, it uses a committed <Term id="fixture">fixture</Term> so it still runs for free — and you can switch on <Term id="byok">BYOK</Term> mode to re-run it live against your own key.

That changes what reading means here. When you hit a runnable cell, the instruction is never "look at this." It is **"change a number and see what moves."** A concept you have perturbed and watched react is one you can rebuild later. A concept you only read is one you will have to look up.

<Callout type="note">
This course is deliberately long — about 88 chapters across 11 layers, months of work at a sustainable pace. That is a feature. The goal is not to finish; it is to still understand chapter 3 by the time you reach chapter 30.
</Callout>

## How it's built

The course is organised as eleven **layers**, from the maths and Python foundations up through classical ML, deep learning, transformers, the application-engineering core, evaluation, adaptation, serving, operations, safety, and finally specialisations. Each builds on the ones below it.

- **Build** chapters take you all the way down: you implement the thing from scratch, so you understand every moving part.
- **Use** chapters stop at correct, informed usage: you can apply the technique and argue its trade-offs without rebuilding it.
- **Know** chapters are a map pin: you learn that something exists and what problem it solves, so you can reach for it later.

Inside a chapter the shape is always the same — a hook, the intuition in plain words, diagrams, the mechanics, a worked example, something interactive, and then a **"Production reality"** section that most tutorials skip: what breaks, what it costs, and what the trade-offs are. That section is the difference between knowing a technique and being trusted to ship it.

## A worked example: the run–break–tune loop

Here is the loop this course wants to become a habit. Suppose you meet this code:

```python
def average(numbers):
    total = 0
    for n in numbers:
        total += n
    return total / len(numbers)

print(average([]))   # what happens here?
```

Passive reading says "a function that averages a list — fine, next." The run–break–tune loop says: *run it.* You get a `ZeroDivisionError`, because an empty list has length zero. Now you have learned something the prose never told you — this function has an edge case — and you learned it in the strongest possible way, by watching it fail. **Break, observe, then fix.** That single habit, applied a few hundred times across this course, is most of what separates an engineer from a spectator.

## Production reality

No one hands a working AI engineer a tidy curriculum. The way people actually stay current on the job is a rougher version of this course: they **read the source** of the library they depend on, they **break things in a staging environment** to learn the failure modes before production finds them for them, and they keep a running note of trade-offs they have personally hit.

The cost is real and worth naming: **depth is slow.** Genuinely understanding attention, or why a reranker earns its latency, takes hours you could have spent skimming ten blog posts. The blog posts feel more productive. They are not — a week later they are gone, and the person who built one small thing from scratch still has it.

The trade-off you are managing throughout is **breadth versus depth**. You cannot build everything. The depth tags are your budget: spend "build" effort where you will work daily, take "know" depth on the rest, and revisit. Teams lose months to engineers who tutorial-hopped their way to a shallow familiarity with everything and a working command of nothing.

<Callout type="watch-out">
The most expensive mistake in learning this material is the feeling of progress without the substance of it. Finishing a chapter you did not run, or skipping the "Production reality" section because the chapter was already long, gives you the feeling and skips the substance.
</Callout>
